<SectionTitle numeral="I">To Say the Same Thing as God</SectionTitle>

<div className="drop-cap">
There is no chapter in our book on confession, and so I want to interject a class on it — for confession stands at the very threshold of the Christian life. Before a person is baptized in the Orthodox Church, they come to confession; and baptism is joined to chrismation; and the whole of it has one aim — to bring us into **communion with God and with one another** in the Church. That is the point. Apart from that initial confession, we confess in several ways throughout our life in the Church, and the most familiar of them — going to a priest — is only one. But the purpose of every one of them is the same: to remain in communion with God and with one another.
</div>

There are two ways the very word *confess* is used in the Scripture and in the Church, and they answer to two things we confess. One is to confess our **faith**; the other is to confess our **sins**. And the root of the Greek word for *confession* means, simply, *to say the same thing*. Think of the Creed: when we confess our faith, what are we doing? We are agreeing with God — repeating back to God and to one another what He has told us about Himself. We are saying the same thing He says.

So too with sins. In the confession of faith we agree with what God says about *Himself*; in the confession of sins we agree with what God says about *us* — and about where we need to be.

<Scripture cite="Matthew 10:32">
Whoever confesses Me before men, him I will also confess before My Father who is in heaven.
</Scripture>

That is a confession of *faith*. For the other, turn to the First Epistle of John, and notice that the Apostle backs the matter up a verse, into the language of *communion*.

<Scripture cite="1 John 1:7–9">
If we walk in the light as He is in the light, we have fellowship with one another, and the blood of Jesus Christ His Son cleanses us from all sin. If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.
</Scripture>

The word translated *fellowship* is *koinonia* — **communion**. And in one breath the Apostle joins communion with one another, the blood of Christ, and the confession of sins. Any Christian who hears *communion* and *the blood of Christ* in a single verse knows at once what is being spoken of: Holy Communion. And to it John binds the confession of our sins, in the plural — *if we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive*. He goes on: *if anyone sins, we have an Advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous; and He Himself is the propitiation for our sins.* To confess, then — to say the same thing as God — is at once to confess our faith in the Creed and to confess our sins. This is simply the usage of the Church.

<SectionTitle numeral="II">What the Confession of Sins Is</SectionTitle>

What, then, is the confession of sins? It is the exercise of **repentance for reconciliation** with God and with one another — a deliberate acknowledgement of ungodliness *so as to turn away from it*, in forgiveness, toward a righteous life in Christ. It is not merely my admitting what I did wrong. It is admitting what I did wrong *in order to* receive the forgiveness of Christ and the grace of God, that I may now do what is right.

You have heard the saying, *confession is good for the soul*. But if that is all it is — if I simply vent about my sins — it helps nothing. Unless we receive the forgiveness of our sins and the grace of God to overcome them, what is the point? Bare admission, with no turning, becomes only an excuse to sin again. *I'm sorry — how many times have I done this?* You have heard it; so have I. How do you know whether someone is truly sorry? They stop. And how do you know whether someone has truly been forgiven by God? As far as the east is from the west, so far has He removed our transgressions from us — and we do not go on in them.

So confession is a **threefold** reality. It is not the mere admission of sin, nor only the word *you are forgiven* — it is a **change**, a transformation, by the grace of God, so that we are no longer slaves to sin but doers of righteousness. This is crucial, for it is how we know that anything in the Church is real at all: by an actual experience of the grace of God, an actual change taking place.

<SectionTitle numeral="III">To Confess Out Loud</SectionTitle>

Two Greek words express the nature of the confession of sin. The first, the most common in the writings of the Church, is *exomologesis* — to **declare your sins out loud**. It is the very word for confession, with a prefix that means *to confess them out*. The second is *exagoreusis*, and its root is *agora* — the **marketplace**, the public square, the forum. When St. Paul came to Athens he spent his days in the *agora* (Acts 17), down where the people gathered. *Exagoreusis* means to *tell out in the public square*.

Both words, then, carry something public. This corrects a caricature: confession as a man slipping into a little box in the corner, a priest sliding a screen across, a secret transacted in the dark. In the Orthodox Church everyone can see who is going to confession — they stand out in the open with the priest. Not all can hear what is said, but it is no hidden thing. These two words tell us that confession is never merely a privatized affair between me and God; it involves the whole community, the whole Church.

Hear it in the Scriptures. When Achan stole what God had set apart at Jericho, Israel was defeated at Ai and thirty-six men died — because of one man's hidden sin.

<Scripture cite="Joshua 7:19">
Give glory to the Lord God of Israel, and make confession to Him, and tell me now what you have done; do not hide it from me.
</Scripture>

He confesses aloud — *exomologesis* — and the whole community brings the matter to its end in the Valley of Achor. Mark it well: a sin perhaps no one else knew of, yet other men died for it, and it was the *whole community* that dealt with it. Sin, forgiveness, and the setting-right of things always have a corporate dimension. So at the Jordan:

<Scripture cite="Matthew 3:5–6">
Then Jerusalem, all Judea, and all the region around the Jordan went out to him, and were baptized by him in the Jordan, confessing their sins.
</Scripture>

All who came to baptism confessed their sins aloud before they went down into the water. And St. James, speaking of the anointing with oil for the healing of soul and body, uses the same word — and the *plural*. I will give it to you in the Arkansas translation, for the verb is *y'all*:

<Scripture cite="James 5:16">
Confess your trespasses to one another, and pray for one another, that you may be healed. The effective, fervent prayer of a righteous man avails much.
</Scripture>

<SectionTitle numeral="IV">The Witness of the Old Covenant</SectionTitle>

The same corporate confession runs all through the Old Covenant. Under the influence of Western scholasticism the sacraments came to be treated as a *system* — individualized, a private dose of grace for whatever I happen to have done — and the corporate nature of these things was lost. Not so in the Scriptures. The **trespass offering** is by definition a public act: you bring the animal, it is slaughtered, it is burned upon the altar — and the confession of sin is made together with it.

<Scripture cite="Leviticus 5:5">
And it shall be, when he is guilty in any of these matters, that he shall confess that he has sinned in that thing.
</Scripture>

Greater still is the **Day of Atonement**. Two goats were brought. One was slain, and its blood carried within the veil — the one day of the year the high priest entered the Holy of Holies, where the Ark stood. The other goat received the sins of the people:

<Scripture cite="Leviticus 16:21">
Aaron shall lay both his hands on the head of the live goat, confess over it all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their transgressions, concerning all their sins, putting them on the head of the goat, and shall send it away into the wilderness.
</Scripture>

The scapegoat bears the sins of the whole nation and is sent into the wilderness — a place described as *inaccessible*. So on the Day of Atonement two things happen at once: the people are given **access** to God in the Holiest Place, while their sins are **sent away** to a place they can never reach again. St. Paul takes this up in Hebrews and applies it to Holy Communion. Every Divine Liturgy is a Day of Atonement: through the blood of Christ we are given access to the Holiest Place behind the veil, and through that same blood our sins are sent away, no longer accessible to us.

And it is confession the whole congregation makes together. So Nehemiah:

<Scripture cite="Nehemiah 9:2">
Then those of Israelite lineage separated themselves from all foreigners; and they stood and confessed their sins and the iniquities of their fathers.
</Scripture>

A whole nation, confessing — even *the iniquities of their fathers*. Not because guilt is inherited as a curse to be punished, but because the children had **perpetuated** the ungodliness handed down to them; they confess the sins they themselves committed and the sins their fathers taught them to walk in. And even the lone voice of the Psalmist confesses in the midst of the assembly, before the altar:

<Scripture cite="Psalm 31:5 (LXX)">
I acknowledged my sin to You, and my iniquity I have not hidden. I said, "I will confess my transgressions to the Lord," and You forgave the iniquity of my sin.
</Scripture>

People object to the Orthodox practice — *why must I confess before a priest? Why can I not simply confess to God?* But you can. Only consider the *setting* of nearly every passage we have read: the confession is made in the assembly, before the altar. There is a corporate nature to sin, to forgiveness, and to reconciliation. Ours is not an *either/or* religion but a *both/and*: confession to God, and confession in the body — all of the above.

<SectionTitle numeral="V">Inherited Sin, Not Inherited Guilt</SectionTitle>

A word, since it arises, on what we do and do not inherit. We are not held *guilty* of our fathers' sins, as though we were punished for them. This is a real difference between the Orthodox understanding and others. God does not account us guilty and damned for what is often called *original sin*. We are not condemned at the moment of our conception. But it is because of the **corruption of our flesh** — inherited from Adam — that we die; and because of that decay and sickness, human beings will inevitably come to sin personally. This is why we baptize infants: not because they are guilty of personal sins, for they are not, but because all of us are born into a flesh corrupted by the corruption of sin, and they need the forgiveness and the healing of Christ as we all do. We do *not* say that one who dies unbaptized is automatically damned — that would be inherited *guilt* — yet we do bring even the infants to Christ.

So too in the Old Covenant the sign of the saving relationship was **circumcision** — the mark that these are the people in a living covenant with God. But circumcision was never *magic*; had it been, the little girls, who were uncircumcised, would have been lost — and they were not, for they too were included in the covenant. The sign showed *which people* belonged to God and sought to do what pleases Him. Baptism is the same. It is not magic either; yet Christ commands it, for it is the appointed way in which He forgives our sins and gives us the Holy Spirit — and the grace then given is that we might join our will to His and be truly changed, beginning a whole life of spiritual maturity.

<SectionTitle numeral="VI">The Four Ways We Confess</SectionTitle>

There are four chief ways in which we confess in the Church. The first I have already named: **confession before baptism and chrismation** — before a person is received into the Church and comes to the chalice, he confesses his sins.

The second is **confession in the Divine Liturgy**, every week. What is the prayer we say just before we approach to receive?

<Canon source="The Prayer Before Communion">
I believe, O Lord, and I confess that Thou art truly the Christ, the Son of the living God, who camest into the world to save sinners, of whom I am first. I believe also that this is truly Thine own most pure Body, and that this is truly Thine own precious Blood. Therefore I pray Thee: have mercy upon me and forgive my transgressions, both voluntary and involuntary, of word and of deed, committed in knowledge and in ignorance; and make me worthy to partake without condemnation of Thy most pure Mysteries, for the remission of sins and unto life everlasting.
</Canon>

Is that not a confession? And who prays it? Everyone who is about to commune — and we pray it *together*, in the liturgical assembly. To come to Communion presupposes that we are *living* in confession, living in repentance and forgiveness, applying ourselves to the opposite of our sins, to what is pleasing in God's sight. How many of us have ever thought that simply coming to the Liturgy is itself an act of confession? For when Christ says *given and shed for you for the forgiveness of sins* — *take, eat* — to whom is He speaking? To the baptized, gathered for the Liturgy, coming in repentance. This does not mean one must be sinless to commune, nor that we are turned away for the sins all of us contend with daily. It means precisely that *this* is why we have Communion: we come confessing our sins, to receive forgiveness, in a continual renovation of life. So the Apostle:

<Scripture cite="Hebrews 10:22–25">
Let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, having our hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water. Let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering… and let us consider one another in order to stir up love and good works, not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together.
</Scripture>

*Our bodies washed with pure water* — there is baptism; and the *confession of our hope*, held fast in the assembly. The Divine Liturgy is itself an act of corporate confession, where all come to the one cup together; and so there can be nothing left standing between a man and God, or between a man and his neighbor, when he comes.

<OrnamentDivider variant="section" />

<SectionTitle numeral="VII">Confession with One Another</SectionTitle>

This brings us to the third way: **confession to each other**. The Church assumes we are living it — especially when we come to Communion. Hear the Lord in the Sermon on the Mount:

<Scripture cite="Matthew 5:23–24">
Therefore if you bring your gift to the altar, and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar, and go your way. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift.
</Scripture>

To come to the Divine Liturgy with everyone presupposes that we come in peace with everyone. There is an ancient order in which all greeted one another with a holy kiss before the Creed — the **Kiss of Peace**, which the clergy still exchange — and if two were at odds and would not greet each other, the bishop would come out and they had to be reconciled *on the spot* for the Liturgy to go on. The Liturgy does not continue until we are at peace with one another. We keep a remnant of this once a year in the **Rite of Mutual Forgiveness** at the start of Lent, when every person asks and gives forgiveness to every other. It can harden into a formula — *forgive me, a sinner* — but where there is a real grievance it must be specific: *I am sorry for harming you; please forgive me.* That is what ought to be happening all the time.

And when a brother sins against us, the Lord gives the order of it:

<Scripture cite="Matthew 18:15–17">
Moreover if your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault between you and him alone. If he hears you, you have gained your brother. But if he will not hear, take with you one or two more, that by the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established. And if he refuses to hear them, tell it to the church. But if he refuses even to hear the church, let him be to you like a heathen and a tax collector.
</Scripture>

If it is not resolved between the two, bring one or two others; if still not, *tell it to the church* — the *ekklesia*, the liturgical assembly. And then He repeats what He had said to Peter in chapter 16, but now in the plural:

<Scripture cite="Matthew 18:18">
Assuredly, I say to you, whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven.
</Scripture>

To **bind** a sin is to leave it attached to a man, so that he bears its consequence; to **loose** it is to forgive it. The Lord says the same after the Resurrection: *whatever sins you retain are retained, whatever sins you forgive are forgiven* (John 20). And what becomes of us if we refuse to let go of our sin in the end? We are separated from God. That is how serious the binding and loosing of sin is. Yet the measure of our forgiving has no limit:

<Scripture cite="Matthew 18:21–22">
Then Peter came to Him and said, "Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Up to seven times?" Jesus said to him, "I do not say to you, up to seven times, but up to seventy times seven."
</Scripture>

And again, very practically:

<Scripture cite="Luke 17:3–4">
If your brother sins against you, rebuke him; and if he repents, forgive him. And if he sins against you seven times in a day, and seven times in a day returns to you, saying, "I repent," you shall forgive him.
</Scripture>

This is no abstraction. Do you acknowledge it to a good friend when you have wronged him, and ask his pardon? Husbands and wives must practice confession and absolution with one another, or it ends in disaster; and so with our children. The Apostle James warns that whoever stumbles in one point of the law is guilty of all — meaning we cannot **bargain** with God over sin. *I commit adultery, but I give much to the poor; surely it balances out.* No — not if you mean to go on in adultery unrepentant, for that is incompatible with Christ, the Holy One. We are not rejected from the chalice for the sins we struggle against in a basic attitude of repentance, humility, and faith; but there are sins which, clung to without repentance, do separate us from the communion of God.

<SectionTitle numeral="VIII">Confession Before the Cross: The Mystery</SectionTitle>

The fourth way is what most people first think of when they hear the word — **confession before the cross**, what we call the **Mystery** (or Sacrament) of Confession. We name it a *mystery* because bishops and priests are stewards of the mysteries of God, and this is a particular way in which *Christ in you, the hope of glory* is made manifest in the lives of Christians. It belongs to the fact that the bishops and priests are the shepherds and pastors of the Church.

For consider: when a grievous sin is committed after baptism — one that threatens to separate a man from the communion of God — how is it to be dealt with? By the very path Matthew 18 lays out: you work your way up, and at the last you *tell it to the Church*. And who leads and shepherds the Church? The apostles, before they died, appointed **bishops and priests** to succeed them in shepherding the flock.

<Scripture cite="Acts 20:28">
Take heed to yourselves and to all the flock, among which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to shepherd the church of God which He purchased with His own blood.
</Scripture>

St. Peter charges the elders to *shepherd the flock of God among you* (1 Peter 5), and St. Paul leaves Titus to *set in order* what is lacking and to appoint elders in every city (Titus 1). It is to these shepherds that the healing of one caught in serious sin is entrusted, and the Mystery has **three** parts:

- First, the **admission of sin** — the confession itself, named and brought into the light.
- Second, the **pronouncement of forgiveness**, sometimes called **absolution** — the loosing of the sin in the name of Christ.
- Third, the *epitimia*, usually rendered **penance** — though *penance* carries a wrong connotation. It comes from a Latin word for *paying a fine*, and that is exactly what this is *not*. In the Orthodox Church it is no fine, no satisfying of a debt, but a **medicine** prescribed by the physician of souls for the healing of the one who has been wounded by sin — a prayer rule, a fast, an almsgiving, suited to draw the heart back into health.

The Mystery of Confession, then, is not a transaction at a charging station — *plug in, draw the grace you need, and go your way*. The Western sacrament was conceived as a *channel* of grace, an infusion. For us, the Mystery is a **manifestation** and a **healing**: the binding and loosing the Lord entrusted to His apostles, worked out among us so that the one who has fallen may be restored to the communion of God and of the Church, and truly changed.

<SectionTitle numeral="IX">Why We Confess At All</SectionTitle>

Why must we be this honest with God, with the priest, with one another? Because we have made religion such a privatized thing that it has grown strange to us — *must I really be that truthful, and that forgiving? Why can I not simply show up for an hour and a half, get what I came for, and go live my life?* It does not work that way; not in Christ. The main body of the church is called the **nave** — from the word for *ship* — because we are all traveling together in one vessel, in the same direction. Our life with Christ is necessarily bound up with the life of every other person who is in communion with God.

So we confess not only specifically but generally: *I am a sinner.* We have no defense before God if we come to justify our sin. To be holy is not to be sinless — none of us could ever come on that condition — but to be *set apart for God*, and to come in repentance, humility, and faith, receiving the forgiveness and the grace by which we are truly changed. This is the whole reason the Lord gave us His Supper: that we who confess our sins might receive His forgiveness, and live.
