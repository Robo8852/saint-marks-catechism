<SectionTitle numeral="I">Pentecost: The Fulfillment of Pascha</SectionTitle>

<div className="drop-cap">
We come now to the seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit. Open to Acts chapter two — for so much of what we say goes back to Acts; we are not making it up. The feast of Pentecost takes its name from the *fiftieth day* after Pascha. Its older name in the Old Testament is the **Feast of Weeks**. At the Passover they would put the sickle to the grain and bring the first fruits of the harvest; then they counted seven weeks, and on the fiftieth day kept Pentecost, a harvest festival for the gathering in of the grain.
</div>

Notice the connection. Christ is called the *first fruits* in the great Resurrection chapter — and the first fruits lead to the fullness of the harvest. What was a sign in the Old Testament is now fulfilled in the Person of Jesus Christ, and in the Church.

<Scripture cite="Acts 2:1–4">
When the Day of Pentecost had fully come, they were all with one accord in one place. And suddenly there came a sound from heaven, as of a rushing mighty wind… and there appeared to them divided tongues, as of fire, and one sat upon each of them. And they were all filled with the Holy Spirit.
</Scripture>

It does not say tongues *of* fire, but tongues *as of* fire — like fire. The word for *spirit* in both Hebrew and Greek is the same word Jesus elsewhere translates as *wind*. "The wind blows where it wishes; you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So is everyone who is born of the Spirit." You cannot see where the Spirit comes from or goes — but you can tell where He is by where He is *blowing*. Before His Passion the Lord had promised exactly this: *"I am going away… but I will send you another Comforter, another Paraclete, and He will lead you into all truth."* And so He is purposely present at Pentecost as the fulfillment of all that Christ accomplished by His Cross and Resurrection.

This is why in the Orthodox Church we also call Pentecost **Holy Trinity Day**. Here is the full manifestation of who God is: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The Father sends the Son in the flesh, who is crucified and raised and ascends to the Father; and now the Father and the Son together give the Holy Spirit — the whole Holy Trinity working in completion of God's plan of salvation for us sinners.

<SectionTitle numeral="II">Fire and Tongue</SectionTitle>

Why fire? In the Old Testament God describes Himself as a **consuming fire**. When the people came to Mount Sinai, the top of the mountain appeared engulfed in flame; the smoke went up as from a furnace. Fire has two properties: it burns, refines, and purifies — and it gives off light and warmth. What does God want to burn up? Whatever is impure and corrupt and sinful. What does He want to enlighten? Us, in the truth.

This is how the same God is experienced as both heaven and hell. Heaven is the experience of God as a purifying light; hell is the experience of that very same God as a burning fire.

And why a *tongue*? As St. Gregory the Theologian says, it is by the tongue that we speak words. The Son of God speaks the Word of God, and the Holy Spirit enables *us* to speak the Word of God — so the tongue of fire shows the bond between the Holy Spirit and the Son. The apostles, all Galileans, spoke at once, yet every man heard them in his own language. This is no ordinary "speaking in tongues" — it is plainly an act of God, given for a single purpose: to call people to repentance and faith in Christ.

<SectionTitle numeral="III">The Pattern: Repent, Be Baptized, Receive the Gift</SectionTitle>

When Peter finished preaching, the people were cut to the heart and cried, *"What shall we do?"*

<Scripture cite="Acts 2:38–39">
Repent, and let every one of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins; and you shall receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. For the promise is to you and to your children, and to all who are afar off, as many as the Lord our God will call.
</Scripture>

Here is the whole pattern. *Repent* — in Greek *metanoia*, a complete change of mind — then be baptized for the forgiveness of sins, and receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. And those who were baptized "continued steadfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and in the prayers." Repentance and confession lead to baptism and forgiveness, which are followed by the gift of the Spirit, which leads to Communion.

This is exactly the order we still follow. In the Orthodox Church baptism — especially of adults — is preceded by confession, and immediately followed by **Chrismation**, the sealing with the gift of the Holy Spirit; and the newly baptized and chrismated then receive Holy Communion. Baptized, sealed, communed — all together.

The West rearranged this. Somewhere in the Middle Ages the Roman Catholics separated confirmation from baptism, so that today an infant is baptized, receives Communion around age seven, and is confirmed only in adolescence. Most Protestants discarded chrismation and confirmation altogether — because by their definition a sacrament must have an explicit command of Christ joined to a physical element, and they could not find where Jesus commanded anointing with oil. So confirmation became a mere instructional exercise, emptied of any physical sealing.

<Canon>
Our theology is not a different set of conclusions reached by the same method. It is a different method. The Roman Catholics and the Protestants used the same words as the early Church, but filled them with a different meaning.
</Canon>

People say the only difference is the papacy. No — the whole *way* of doing theology differs. The West became wedded to logical categories. Yet the Orthodox are the truly *logical* ones, for *logikos* comes from *Logos*, the Word: St. Paul calls our worship our *logikē latreia*, our reasonable, word-shaped service. For us, to be logical means to be in accord with the Word of God; for another tradition it means in accord with one's own categories of thought.

<SectionTitle numeral="IV">Recapitulation: All Things Gathered Up in Christ</SectionTitle>

Do we still need to be baptized in water? Yes — not merely because Christ commanded it, but because this is the very way in which God brings us in and assures us He is at work. The pattern of water runs through all of Scripture: the Flood; the river Ezekiel saw rising from the temple; the dove that returned to Noah, and the same Spirit descending as a dove upon Christ. As an Orthodox priest once put it: when Christ was baptized He was not washed by the water — He **washed the water**, that going forward our old nature might die in it and we come alive to Christ.

In Ephesians 1, verses three through fourteen are a single sentence in Greek, and its main verb stands in verse ten:

<Scripture cite="Ephesians 1:9–10">
…that in the dispensation of the fullness of the times He might gather together in one all things in Christ, both which are in heaven and which are on earth — in Him.
</Scripture>

"Gather together in one" is the Greek *anakephalaiōsis* — to bring everything up *into the head*. This is **recapitulation**: all things summed up and gathered into Christ the Head. The letter to the Hebrews says the same thing in another way:

<Scripture cite="Hebrews 8:1–2">
Now this is the main point of the things we are saying: We have such a High Priest, who is seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens, a Minister of the sanctuary and of the true tabernacle which the Lord erected, and not man.
</Scripture>

The word there for *Minister* is *leitourgos*, liturgist — the work of the people; and Hebrews speaks of the new covenant, which Christ inaugurates at the Lord's Supper. We have such a low view of what Christ accomplished. We have more faith in what Adam did than in what Christ has done. Recall **Naaman the Syrian**: an accomplished general who hid his leprosy beneath his armor, and was healed only by dipping seven times in the lowly Jordan. So with us — we keep gathering accolades and going deeper into religious performance, all the while covering a body that is dying, until we receive the true medicine of the soul.

<SectionTitle numeral="V">Persons, Not Individuals: Theosis or Idolatry</SectionTitle>

To speak of chrismation is not to speak of a sacramental machine — pull the right levers and you are a Christian in good standing. It is to speak of **communion** with God the Holy Trinity, and with one another. For in Orthodoxy everything is *personal*. God is three Persons in one essence; there is no God apart from the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, who never act independently of one another but exist only in total mutual self-giving. And just so, there is no such thing as a Christian apart from communion with God and with the brethren.

Consider the very word *individual*: it means that which is *divided off by itself*. The Greek is *atomos*, an atom — the old Epicurean notion that the universe is only particles bouncing off one another and occasionally cohering by chance. Does that sound like anything you heard in science class? Applied to human beings and to God, it makes us self-contained units — the exact opposite of how God made us, and of who God is.

This is why Pentecost is the undoing of **Babel**. At the tower, men sought to become gods by their own grasping, and their tongues were divided; at Pentecost, through Christ, the tongues are reunited. There are finally only two possibilities for any human being before God: either you become like God *by grace*, sharing His divine life — which is *theosis* — or you set out to become your own god, which is idolatry. The Church has iconography; the world has idolatry. "I said, you are gods," our Lord quotes from the Psalm. We are not God by nature, but by grace we are called to partake of the divine life.

<SectionTitle numeral="VI">Anointing and Sealing: The Witness of Scripture</SectionTitle>

How did the first Christians know what the apostles meant when they spoke of being *anointed* and *sealed*? Because it had actually been done to them.

<Scripture cite="2 Corinthians 1:21–22">
Now He who establishes us with you in Christ and has anointed us is God, who also has sealed us and given us the Spirit in our hearts as a guarantee.
</Scripture>

Two joined words: *anointing* and *sealing*. The Corinthians knew this firsthand — oil had been put on them, and by that physical anointing God showed that the Holy Spirit had been given. The two are not mutually exclusive; the verse holds both together.

<Scripture cite="Ephesians 1:13–14">
In Him you also trusted, after you heard the word of truth… in whom also, having believed, you were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise, who is the guarantee of our inheritance until the redemption of the purchased possession.
</Scripture>

<Scripture cite="1 John 2:20, 27">
But you have an anointing from the Holy One, and you know all things… But the anointing which you have received from Him abides in you, and you do not need that anyone teach you; but as the same anointing teaches you concerning all things, and is true, and is not a lie… you will abide in Him.
</Scripture>

The word for *anointing* there is *chrism*. How could the people know what John meant unless they had received the chrism? This is why, at the end of the Orthodox service of baptism and chrismation, we perform the **ablution** — we wash the chrism off. Why wash off what was just applied? To show that chrismation is more than the outward anointing of oil: it is the inner gift of the Holy Spirit, who remains even when the oil is gone. You will go home and take a shower; you may go to the beach. The chrism washes away — the Spirit does not.

<SectionTitle numeral="VII">Kings and Priests: The Chrism in the Old Testament</SectionTitle>

The words gathered here all belong together: *chrism*, the anointing oil; *Christ*, the Anointed One; *Christian*, a little anointed one; *chrismation*, the applying of the chrism. In the Old Testament both priests and kings were anointed, and both were therefore called *christs* — anointed ones.

<Scripture cite="Exodus 29:6–9">
You shall put the turban on his head… and you shall take the anointing oil, pour it on his head, and anoint him. Then you shall bring his sons… and the priesthood shall be theirs for a perpetual statute.
</Scripture>

So Aaron and his sons were consecrated with the holy chrism. And the kings were anointed too — Samuel took a vial of oil and poured it on Saul's head, the first king of Israel; and later, when Saul had forfeited his throne by disobedience, Samuel anointed the youngest son of Jesse:

<Scripture cite="1 Kingdoms 16:12–13 (LXX)">
And the Lord said, Arise, anoint David, for he is the one. Then Samuel took the horn of oil and anointed him in the midst of his brothers; and the Spirit of the Lord came upon David from that day forward.
</Scripture>

Both offices — king and priest — are fulfilled in the one Person of Jesus Christ: the King of the line of David, and (as Hebrews shows) the great High Priest. And now *we Christians* are the little anointed ones, the little christs, sealed with the chrism — and so we too are called kings and priests:

<Scripture cite="Revelation 1:5–6">
To Him who loved us and washed us from our sins in His own blood, and has made us kings and priests to His God and Father — to Him be glory and dominion forever and ever. Amen.
</Scripture>

In Revelation the faithful are *sealed* on the forehead. The image comes from Ezekiel 9, where the angel is told to set a mark upon the foreheads of those who have not gone after idols. In the Hebrew the word is *tau* — the last letter of the alphabet — and a *tau* was written as a cross. So when we seal a newly chrismated Christian, tracing the cross upon the brow, eyes, nostrils, mouth, and ears, we say: *"The seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit."*

<SectionTitle numeral="VIII">Gifts and Fruits of the Holy Spirit</SectionTitle>

As St. Nicholas Cabasilas writes, in baptism we are given new birth, and in chrismation the **gifts and energies of the Holy Spirit are activated** within us. We do not merely receive the things of Christ; we receive *Christ Himself*. But there is a difference between the *gifts* of the Spirit and the *fruits* of the Spirit — and the Corinthians confused the two, imagining that flashier gifts, such as tongues, marked out a superior Christian.

<Scripture cite="1 Corinthians 12:4–7">
There are diversities of gifts, but the same Spirit. There are differences of ministries, but the same Lord. And there are diversities of activities, but it is the same God who works all in all. But the manifestation of the Spirit is given to each one for the profit of all.
</Scripture>

The word for *activities* is *energēmata*, energies. Yet these very Corinthians, so enamored of their gifts, were divided and envious — still enslaved to the basic passions. So the possession of gifts is not the pinnacle of the Christian life. "Pursue love," Paul says, "and desire spiritual gifts, but especially that you may prophesy" — for prophecy is given not to predict the future for show, but to build up the body of Christ in repentance and faith. A gift is something still to be brought to **maturity**.

Think of an infant who inherits a fortune. He is truly the heir — he owns it all — but he comes into real possession only when he grows up and learns to use it rightly. (This is why most who inherit a great fortune lose it within a few years.) As St. Paul says, there is no difference between an heir and a slave except maturity. So the question is not what gifts we can display, but what we are becoming:

<Scripture cite="Galatians 5:22–24">
But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control. Against such there is no law. And those who are Christ's have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires.
</Scripture>

There is the true violence — *the violent take the kingdom by force* — not violence done to others, but the effort by which a Christian crucifies the flesh. The fruits are the gifts brought to maturity, the image of Christ grown into His likeness. And this whole growth begins in baptism and chrismation.

<SectionTitle numeral="IX">The True Test: Fruit, Not Signs</SectionTitle>

Do the gifts of the Spirit continue? They do — healings, the raising of the dead, the casting out of demons. Even the bodies of departed saints retain them; we call such bodies **relics**. So it was that a dead man, hastily cast into the prophet's tomb, came to life the moment he touched **Elisha's bones**:

<Scripture cite="4 Kingdoms 13:21 (LXX)">
So it was, as they were burying a man, that suddenly they spied a band of raiders; and they put the man in the tomb of Elisha. When the man touched the bones of Elisha, he revived and stood up on his feet.
</Scripture>

The same grace works still. In our own day the energies of the Holy Spirit shone in **St. Gabriel of Georgia** (Urgebadze), the fool-for-Christ. And the myrrh-streaming icons bear the same witness: the **Hawaiian Iveron Icon** of the Theotokos was touched to the **Kardiotissa Icon** now kept in Taylor, Pennsylvania, which has since streamed **myrrh** continuously for years — thousands come, thousands of miracles are documented — and it ceases to stream every Holy Week, then begins again at Pascha. How do you explain that?

But why does God give such signs at all? As the Fathers say, **miracles are given chiefly to those who are weak in faith**. For the strong, it is not about miracles; it is about the unbroken communion we have with God in the Holy Spirit, whose fruit is love, joy, peace, and patience. The ultimate proof of the Spirit is not a supernatural gift — for gifts can be imitated and counterfeited.

<Scripture cite="1 Corinthians 13:8">
Love never fails. But whether there are prophecies, they will fail; whether there are tongues, they will cease; whether there is knowledge, it will vanish away.
</Scripture>

This is why Scripture warns of false signs and wonders. In Revelation, everything given to the saints is counterfeited by Satan: against the Father stands the dragon, against the slain-yet-living Lamb the beast with its mortal wound healed, against the Holy Spirit the false prophet who works signs — a false trinity. The saints are sealed on the forehead and the right hand with the seal of God; the counterfeit is the *mark of the beast*, set on forehead and right hand. The devil himself appears as an angel of light. So how do we tell the difference? By the experience of communion with the Holy Trinity, and by the goal to which the gifts are put — the fruit of the Spirit.

That is how you know a man is a Christian: not that he works wonders, but that he can pray for those who persecute him, love those who would be rid of him, and forgive those who do him harm.

In the caves of the **Kyiv Pechersk Lavra** the bodies of the faithful are kept by the thousands — the relics of the saints, interred not as some morbid display but in the sure hope of the resurrection. (It is said that among them the skulls of those who had been priests came, over time, to bear the very form of a cross.)

<Canon>
We do not cremate the body, because it will be raised on the last day, and it is an essential part of the person. To them, a skull is not Halloween; it is part of a person, kept for the resurrection.
</Canon>

And so repentance — *metanoia* — proves to be far more than "behave yourself." It is the complete reorientation of the whole human person to the will of God in Christ. As Father Kenneth used to say: *we are already in paradise; it is only the way we are looking at things that is off.*
