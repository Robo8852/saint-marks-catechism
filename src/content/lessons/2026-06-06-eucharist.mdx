<SectionTitle numeral="I">The Mystical Supper: Names and Meaning</SectionTitle>

<div className="drop-cap">
We come now to the chapter titled the **Mystical Supper** — what we also call the Eucharist, the Lord's Supper, and Holy Communion. *Mystical* does not mean vague or obscure; it means that this supper pertains to the **mystery of Christ**, which is "Christ in you, the hope of glory" — God with us. The Mystical Supper is the very perfection and completion of that mystery. Everything Christ accomplished, which He delivered to the apostles on the night before He was crucified and raised — and which we repeat every week — is now delivered to *us* in the Lord's Supper.
</div>

What, then, is it? Essentially — and this is the heart of it — it is the very **Body and Blood of Christ**, given to us to eat and to drink, for the forgiveness of sins and for communion with God and all the saints.

But it also embraces what we call the **Eucharist**. *Eucharist* is simply the Greek word for *thanksgiving*. "On the night when He was betrayed, He took bread, and when He had **given thanks**, He broke it." So the Church has always called the Lord's Supper the Eucharist — the Thanksgiving — meaning not the bare eating and drinking alone, but all the prayers that surround that action: the entire **Divine Liturgy**. And the whole purpose of those prayers of thanksgiving is that we might receive the Body and Blood of Christ.

This is why, in the teaching and practice of the Church, it is nonsensical for a baptized Orthodox Christian to come to the Divine Liturgy and *not* receive Holy Communion. The very reason we have the Liturgy is to receive the Lord. As we call Sunday the Lord's *Day*, so this is the Lord's *Supper* — it is unique, His own. And we call it **Holy Communion** because of the communion it gives us: with God, and with one another.

<SectionTitle numeral="II">The Night He Was Betrayed</SectionTitle>

Turn to Matthew 26. This is the week of preparing for the Jewish Feast of Passover, and Jesus tells His apostles to go and make ready for Him to eat the Passover. Now mark the timing, for I have said it before and will say it again: this was a **Thursday**, and the actual Passover was the *next* day, Friday. It was on Friday that the Passover lambs would be slain in the temple, and the Passover meal — the eating of the roasted lamb — would be kept Friday night. So what Jesus does on Thursday is *not* the Jewish Passover meal itself. (This is why those Christians who try to re-enact a Jewish Seder are mistaken about what they are doing — the Seder *is* that Friday Passover meal, and that is not what the Lord was keeping.) He had His apostles prepare everything, but the feast itself was still a day away. And this is of the greatest significance: Christ was crucified at the very hour the Passover lambs were being slain in the temple. **He is the Passover Lamb.** What we read here is the evening before.

<Scripture cite="Matthew 26:26–28">
And as they were eating, Jesus took bread, blessed and broke it, and gave it to the disciples and said, "Take, eat; this is My body." Then He took the cup, and gave thanks, and gave it to them, saying, "Drink from it, all of you. For this is My blood of the new covenant, which is shed for many for the remission of sins."
</Scripture>

Notice plainly: the bread — *"this is My body"*; the cup of wine — *"this is My blood of the new covenant"*; and it is *"for the remission of sins."* He then says He will not drink of the fruit of the vine again "until that day when I drink it new with you in My Father's kingdom" — that is, until the fulfillment of all He came to do, ushering in the kingdom by His death and resurrection.

And there is one detail not to be missed. When the Lord foretells the betrayal — "one of you will betray Me" — and each disciple asks, "Lord, is it I?", the text says, "Then **Judas, who was betraying Him**," asked the same. *Betraying* — present tense. The betrayal is already underway; the sin is active even as he sits at the table and is handed the bread. Hold that picture; we will return to it.

Mark 14 gives us the same account, almost word for word — Matthew and Mark run closely together in these passages. But one word repays a closer look. When it says the Lord "took **bread**," the word is the ordinary Greek word for everyday bread, *artos* — leavened bread, bread made with yeast. This is why the Orthodox Church has always used ordinary leavened bread — as did the Roman Catholics themselves, until the ninth century. The West then adopted **unleavened bread**, *azymes*, as part of a wider drift toward re-importing Old Testament forms.

<Canon>
The change to unleavened bread became one of the historic points of division between East and West — together with the claims of the papacy, the *filioque* added to the Creed, and the doctrine of purgatory.
</Canon>

<SectionTitle numeral="III">Do This in Remembrance</SectionTitle>

Luke gives us the same supper, but with details the others omit. He records *two* cups. First:

<Scripture cite="Luke 22:17–18">
Then He took the cup, and gave thanks, and said, "Take this and divide it among yourselves; for I say to you, I will not drink of the fruit of the vine until the kingdom of God comes."
</Scripture>

Notice that of *this* first cup He does not say, "This is My blood." This is a Jewish ritual meal, in which more than one cup was passed — we know this from history. Only afterward comes the cup of the new covenant:

<Scripture cite="Luke 22:19–20">
And He took bread, gave thanks and broke it, and gave it to them, saying, "This is My body which is given for you; do this in remembrance of Me." Likewise He also took the cup after supper, saying, "This cup is the new covenant in My blood, which is shed for you."
</Scripture>

Dwell on that phrase, *"in remembrance of Me,"* for it is a deeply Jewish thing to say. The Greek is *anamnesis*. In the Old Testament we read that "God remembered Noah," that God "remembered" His people in their bondage in Egypt. Is this merely a mental picture passing through the mind of God? No — when God remembers, *something happens*. For God, to remember is an active verb, not the idle recollection of someone absent. So when Christ says, "Do this in remembrance of Me," He means the apostles are to do what He is doing in order to **participate** in the very life and ministry of Christ Himself. *Anamnesis* is not a re-enactment; it is a participation — a communion. What Christ does here is made real and present among the apostles whenever they do this.

Luke alone preserves what came next, and it matters. Even at this table a dispute arose among them as to which should be considered the greatest:

<Scripture cite="Luke 22:25–27">
The kings of the Gentiles exercise lordship over them, and those who exercise authority over them are called "benefactors." But not so among you; on the contrary, he who is greatest among you, let him be as the younger, and he who governs as he who serves. For who is greater, he who sits at the table, or he who serves? Is it not he who sits at the table? Yet I am among you as the One who serves.
</Scripture>

Even as He institutes the Supper, the Lord teaches that His kingdom is not the lordship of the Gentiles but the way of the servant — for He Himself is among them as the One who serves. And then He bestows that kingdom upon them:

<Scripture cite="Luke 22:28–30">
But you are those who have continued with Me in My trials. And I bestow upon you a kingdom, just as My Father bestowed one upon Me, that you may eat and drink at My table in My kingdom, and sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel.
</Scripture>

This is why we open the Divine Liturgy with the words, *"Blessed is the kingdom of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit"* — for Christ Himself here calls the Lord's Supper His **kingdom**, and the apostles are seated at His table within it. The Mystical Supper is the revelation, the manifestation, of the kingdom of God in Christ, who is about to be crucified and raised. And how do we know it is more than a mental memorial, a bare re-enactment? Because He does not say, "This represents My body." He says, *"Take, eat; this is My body… drink, this is My blood."* It is a real participation, a communion in Christ.

<SectionTitle numeral="IV">The Witness of the Fathers</SectionTitle>

This is no later development. Hear St. Ignatius of Antioch, who was martyred in the year 107 — a man who knew the apostles.

<Canon source="St. Ignatius of Antioch, to the Philadelphians">
Be careful, therefore, to use one Eucharist; for there is one flesh of our Lord Jesus Christ, and one cup for the union with His blood; one altar, as there is one bishop with the presbytery and the deacons, my fellow servants — that whatever you do, you may do it according to God.
</Canon>

And to the Smyrnaeans, of those who had fallen away from the faith, he writes that they hold themselves apart from the Church's worship for one reason above all:

<Canon source="St. Ignatius of Antioch, to the Smyrnaeans">
They abstain from the Eucharist and from prayer, because they do not confess that the Eucharist is the flesh of our Savior Jesus Christ, who suffered for our sins, which the Father raised up by His goodness. They then who deny the gift of God are perishing in their disputes.
</Canon>

Hear also St. Justin Martyr, in his *First Apology*, written to the emperor in the second century:

<Canon source="St. Justin Martyr, First Apology">
We do not receive these as common bread and common drink. But as Jesus Christ our Savior, having been made flesh by the Word of God, had both flesh and blood for our salvation, so likewise we have been taught that the food which is blessed by the prayer of His word, and from which our blood and flesh by transmutation are nourished, is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh.
</Canon>

There has never been a time in the Christian Church when she did not believe and know that what we receive in the Lord's Supper is the very Body and Blood of Christ. This is the revelation of the kingdom; this is where all the work of Christ is imparted to us, week by week. It is exactly what St. Paul means when he tells the Corinthians, "When you come together as a church… I received from the Lord that which I also delivered to you."

<SectionTitle numeral="V">The Bread of Life</SectionTitle>

Turn now to John 6, which follows directly upon the feeding of the five thousand. The crowds come rushing to find Jesus, and He tells them they seek Him not because they understood the sign but because they ate the loaves and were filled. The feeding was a **sign** of something greater. When the Jews murmur — "Is this not Jesus, the son of Joseph? How can He say, 'I have come down from heaven'?" — they have struck the very heart of the Incarnation: the Word became flesh and dwelt among us. He is the God-man.

<Scripture cite="John 6:48–51">
I am the bread of life. Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and are dead. This is the bread which comes down from heaven, that one may eat of it and not die. I am the living bread which came down from heaven. If anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever; and the bread that I shall give is My flesh, which I shall give for the life of the world.
</Scripture>

The Jews then quarrel among themselves: "How can this Man give us His flesh to eat?" They plainly understood that He was *not* speaking figuratively — He meant His actual flesh. And rather than soften it, the Lord presses harder:

<Scripture cite="John 6:53–56">
Most assuredly, I say to you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink His blood, you have no life in you. Whoever eats My flesh and drinks My blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day. For My flesh is food indeed, and My blood is drink indeed. He who eats My flesh and drinks My blood abides in Me, and I in him.
</Scripture>

Notice: this is spoken in the synagogue, not at the table — so it is not yet the Lord's Supper. The question it forces upon us is, *where, then, does He give us His flesh and blood to eat and drink?* Precisely there, with the apostles, on the night He was betrayed. And notice what hangs upon it: *"I will raise him up at the last day."* This is how we come to have life in Christ.

Yet we must not imagine we are receiving a mere chunk of meat. In the same discourse the Lord guards us against any carnal misunderstanding:

<Scripture cite="John 6:63">
It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh profits nothing. The words that I speak to you are spirit, and they are life.
</Scripture>

It is the Holy Spirit who makes the change, and the Holy Spirit who enables us truly to partake of the flesh and blood of Christ. At this, "many of His disciples went back and walked with Him no more." So He turned to the twelve: "Do you also want to go away?"

<Scripture cite="John 6:68–69">
Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life. Also we have come to believe and know that You are the Christ, the Son of the living God.
</Scripture>

And even here the Lord adds, "one of you is a devil" — for He knew from the beginning who would betray Him.

<SectionTitle numeral="VI">How the Change Takes Place</SectionTitle>

People ask the Orthodox: how does it become the Body and Blood of Christ? Is it still bread and wine? Yes — it is bread and wine, and yet it is the Body and Blood, because Jesus says, "This is My body… this is My blood." The Fathers use some thirty different words for what happens, but the most common is the simplest: it is **changed** into the Body and Blood of Christ. We do not conceive of this in a carnal, merely bodily way — as though we were eating a piece of meat and drinking blood drawn from a vein. The Fathers say that if a man looks into the chalice and thinks he sees actual flesh and blood, he is receiving a *deluding vision*. It is the Body and Blood of Christ — but not after the manner of butchery.

Is this, then, **transubstantiation**? No — and here it helps to see where that word came from. In the eleventh century a priest named **Berengar** taught that the bread and wine in the Mass merely *represent* the Body and Blood of Christ. The Roman theologians rightly rejected this, but they overcorrected: they said it does not merely represent but *is* the Body and Blood, to the point that it is **no longer bread and wine at all** — the *substance* having been changed, so that only the appearances of bread and wine remain. That is transubstantiation. They swung from one extreme to the other, and neither extreme is Orthodox.

<Canon>
We do not try to explain *how* it happens. We simply hold to what Jesus said. The change takes place not because the priest pronounces a magical incantation over the bread and wine, but because the Holy Spirit changes them.
</Canon>

Nor should we try to narrow the change down to a single instant on the clock — that is the wrong question to ask. The words of institution — "This is My body" — and the calling down of the Holy Spirit belong together with all the prayers; it is the *whole action* that the Lord gave us. The time of the Liturgy is not ordinary time but *kairos*, the opportune time in which God acts; and what is *kairotic* we are content to leave as mystery, rather than pinning it to a moment we can name. And yet this does not make the bread and wine arbitrary: before the words of Christ are spoken over them, they remain simple bread and wine. We cannot merely gather with bread and wine, declare that we believe it to be the Body and Blood, and have it be so. We do what Christ delivered to His apostles to do — no more, and no less — and that has been handed on in every generation, because this is the essential act of the Church.

<SectionTitle numeral="VII">Take, Eat: Against Reservation and Adoration</SectionTitle>

Because Christ did not say merely "This is My body," but "**Take, eat;** this is My body," and "**Drink** of it, all of you," what we do with the Holy Gifts is itself part of the command. In Roman Catholicism there grew up the practice of the **reserved sacrament**: the consecrated Host is set upon a side altar and adored; and at certain seasons it is carried out in a *monstrance* in the **Corpus Christi** ("the Body of Christ") procession, that the people may worship it where it is displayed. But Christ did not say, "Set this aside and gaze upon it." He said, "Take, eat." And so we do precisely that. Whatever remains in the chalice at the end of the Liturgy is reverently *consumed* by the priest or deacon; nothing is poured back into a bottle, nothing is kept on a shelf.

There is, however, bread on the side table that is **not** the Body of Christ — the *antidoron*. The word means "instead of the gift." Before the Liturgy, a portion is cut from the offered loaves and set upon the *diskos* to become the Body of Christ; out of the remaining bread, with which we commemorate the faithful by name, the rest is cut up and blessed. This blessed bread is given especially to those who are *not* communing — the non-Orthodox, the visitors — as a way of saying: we are glad you are here, we want you with us. It is a remnant of the ancient *agape* meal, a sharing of something with everyone present. But it is **plain bread**, not consecrated; and the cup of wine sometimes offered beside it is likewise plain wine. The *antidoron* is a gift and a welcome — yet the true aim, for which everything else is preparation, remains Holy Communion itself.

<OrnamentDivider variant="section" />

<SectionTitle numeral="VIII">The Holy Things for the Holy Ones</SectionTitle>

If the Gifts are truly changed, then they do not change *back*. The Lutheran church I once served taught that once the service is ended, what remains is no longer the Body and Blood of Christ — it reverts to ordinary wine. But long ago St. Cyril of Alexandria answered such a notion:

<Canon source="St. Cyril of Alexandria">
Christ says it is His Body and Blood — when, then, is it *not*? It does not cease to be what He declared it to be.
</Canon>

This is why we treat the Holy Gifts with such reverence. If there is a spill — as can happen with infants and small children — then, depending on its extent, a stained garment is reverently burned, or a patch of carpet is cut out. You may notice such patches in one of our churches. We are not being superstitious; we are being consistent. Just before Communion the priest lifts up the Gifts and proclaims, *"The holy things for the holy ones."* These are holy things, and we handle them accordingly.

<OrnamentDivider variant="section" />

<SectionTitle numeral="IX">Discerning the Body</SectionTitle>

The fullest teaching on Holy Communion in all the New Testament comes where St. Paul rebukes the Corinthians for abusing it. "When you **come together** as a church," he says — and that phrase, repeated through Acts and the epistles, is the key. The Greek for *church* is *ekklesia*, from which we have *ecclesiastical*: it means the **assembly of the baptized**. And what are they assembled *for*? As Acts tells us, the baptized "continued steadfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and in the prayers." They assemble for the Lord's Supper.

But at Corinth there were *divisions* — hardened into factions, which is the literal sense of the word *heresy*: a party, a sect. Such factions can be doctrinal or moral, and Corinth had grave moral ones (a man living with his father's wife, and worse). So when they gathered, Paul says, it was "not to eat the Lord's Supper," for each rushed ahead with his own supper — "one is hungry and another is drunk." Do you despise the church of God? This is the whole point: if we come together in one place, it must be for the Lord's Supper, not our own. And so he recalls for them the very night of its institution, and shows where it points:

<Scripture cite="1 Corinthians 11:23–26">
For I received from the Lord that which I also delivered to you: that the Lord Jesus, on the same night in which He was betrayed, took bread; and when He had given thanks, He broke it and said, "Take, eat; this is My body which is broken for you; do this in remembrance of Me." In the same manner He also took the cup after supper, saying, "This cup is the new covenant in My blood. This do, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of Me." For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord's death till He comes.
</Scripture>

This is the activity of the Church until the Second Coming of Christ. And precisely because it is the Body and Blood of the Lord, it must not be received carelessly:

<Scripture cite="1 Corinthians 11:27–30">
Therefore whoever eats this bread or drinks this cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of the bread and drink of the cup. For he who eats and drinks in an unworthy manner eats and drinks judgment to himself, not discerning the Lord's body. For this reason many are weak and sick among you, and many sleep.
</Scripture>

"Many sleep" is the Christian way of saying many have *died*. Now consider: if the Body and Blood of Christ were not truly present, why would such consequences fall upon those who profane it? It is **real**. This is why the Church does not let just anyone come and eat. Holy Communion is like medicine from the pharmacist: you do not simply walk up and take what looks good to you, for used wrongly the very thing given for your healing can harm you — even kill you. It is given for our salvation, and we mean to guard it from misuse.

Here the example of **Judas** instructs us. He received the bread along with the other apostles, and Christ knew his heart — yet gave him every opportunity to turn back. So in the Church: when we know that someone is living in *open, unrepentant* sin — a manifest contradiction of what he claims to be in Christ — he is suspended from Communion until there is repentance. If a baptized couple are living together in fornication, unmarried, the priest does not give them Holy Communion until the situation is set right, for they are living in open unrepentance. St. Basil the Great, in his canons, does not necessarily compel such persons to separate, but suspends them from Communion until they are married.

Is this a matter of salvation? Yes, truly:

<Scripture cite="Hebrews 10:26">
For if we sin willfully after we have received the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins.
</Scripture>

And St. Paul warns plainly that those who go on in such things have no share in the kingdom:

<Scripture cite="1 Corinthians 6:9–10">
Do you not know that the unrighteous will not inherit the kingdom of God? Do not be deceived. Neither fornicators, nor idolaters, nor adulterers… nor thieves, nor covetous, nor drunkards… will inherit the kingdom of God.
</Scripture>

One cannot live in open sin against the law of God — murder, adultery, and the like — and suppose that being baptized will carry him into heaven regardless.

And yet — there are also those who come in some hidden condition of heart that we simply cannot see. Christ knew Peter would deny Him three times, and Peter too was at the table. The Church suspends only what is *open* and manifest; the rest she leaves to God, who alone reads the heart.

<SectionTitle numeral="X">Examine Yourself, Not the Age of Reason</SectionTitle>

That same chapter gives us the words, "Let a man **examine himself**, and so let him eat of the bread." Around the year 1000, the West began to read this verb in a new way: to examine oneself, it was said, requires the use of *reason* — therefore only a person who has reached the "age of reason" can receive worthily, and those who have not should be kept from Communion. This was unknown anywhere in the first thousand years of the Church. Yet on its account the Roman Catholics began withholding Communion from newly baptized infants and small children; and the Lutheran church in which I was raised would give Communion to no one until confirmation at around the age of twelve. For the first twelve years of my life I was told I need not come to Communion — even though I longed to.

The Orthodox Church, by contrast, has always given Holy Communion to every baptized person, whatever his age — and whatever his mental state. By the same Western logic, those who fall into dementia, who can no longer give an account of themselves, are turned away. To us this is unthinkable. I have myself given Communion to people in the grip of full-blown Alzheimer's, who could not speak a word — and when the prayers begin, they are *locked in* — for something in them deeper than the failing mind still knows its Lord.

This rationalistic reading found its great expositor in Thomas Aquinas, who taught in his *Summa* that until a person can show proper devotion to the sacrament, he should not be admitted to it. (It is said that toward the end of his life Aquinas counted all such work as straw.) But who among us truly *comprehends* this mystery? When the Lord first gave this teaching, His own disciples said, "This is a hard saying; who can understand it?" If understanding were the test, none of us could come. The problem at Corinth was never that the people were intellectually deficient; it was that they had **no regard for God**. Worthiness is a matter of the heart, not of the head.

<OrnamentDivider variant="section" />

<SectionTitle numeral="XI">One Bread, One Body</SectionTitle>

Back up one chapter, to 1 Corinthians 10, where Paul sets Holy Communion against idolatry. "Flee from idolatry," he says; "I speak as to wise men; judge for yourselves what I say."

<Scripture cite="1 Corinthians 10:16–17">
The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of Christ? For we, though many, are one bread and one body; for we all partake of that one bread.
</Scripture>

The one bread makes us one body. And just as the cup is *communion* in the blood of Christ, so the pagan sacrifice is communion with demons — "you cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons; you cannot partake of the Lord's table and of the table of demons." Communion, then, is no simple act of taking something into the mouth. It is the whole of life. This is why we **prepare** — with the prayers of preparation, and with fasting — for we are coming to receive the Lord who has made us new. And when we leave the church, it is not a matter of having put on nice clothes for an hour and a half and now being free to do as we please. The Liturgy is the clearest manifestation of what we are striving to be every hour of every day.

<OrnamentDivider variant="section" />

<SectionTitle numeral="XII">The Unity of the Church</SectionTitle>

What, then, of Christians outside the Orthodox Church — is there nothing of grace among them? I do not say so. I was a Lutheran pastor for ten years, and I have seen things. There was a man at a Lutheran service who, for all intents and purposes, was dead; we brought Communion to him and gave it to him, and he revived. I witnessed that with my own eyes.

The Orthodox Church recognizes that the Holy Spirit is at work wherever Christ is preached and wherever men are brought to faith in Him. Many are simply doing the best they know how to do. But God is calling everyone to **unity** in the one body of Christ, and whatever gifts of God are given outside the Church are given to draw people *toward* that unity. So there is a spectrum. Some denominations have made it their official doctrine that this is *not* the Body of Christ — and of them the Orthodox would simply say, they do not have it. But others — Roman Catholicism, the Lutheran church of my youth — never denied it; there was never a time when I did not believe it to be truly the Body of Christ. Of them one may say: perhaps you do have it. And yet even so, God is calling us all to unity in one Church.

At the same time, the Holy Spirit cannot be divided against Himself; some groups that call themselves Christian plainly are not, and there the answer is no.

<Scripture cite="Ephesians 4:4–5">
There is one body and one Spirit, just as you were called in one hope of your calling; one Lord, one faith, one baptism.
</Scripture>

This is why the Church cannot simply admit everyone to the chalice — "you are a Christian, come along." To do so would be to **enshrine division** in the very body of Christ: division on doctrinal levels, on moral levels, all of it. And Communion is not there for that. It is not a matter of an unworthy communicant *polluting* what is holy — Holy Communion is holy. It is simply that we do not want the body of Christ to be divided.

<OrnamentDivider variant="section" />

<SectionTitle numeral="XIII">Holiness Is Not Sinlessness</SectionTitle>

Must one go to confession before *every* Communion? Not every time. It depends: if a person has fallen into some grievous sin that has cut him off from Christ, then confession is part of the way he is restored. But ordinarily, no — the assumption is that if you are baptized into Christ and sealed with the gift of the Holy Spirit, this is exactly where Christ wants you: at His table, receiving the Body of the Lord. For we are all contending with sins all the time; but not all sins separate us from Christ. We are still *in* Christ — and that is a vital distinction to make.

So you do not have to be sinless to come to Holy Communion. If that were the rule, none of us would ever come. What, then, does it even mean to be **holy**? The Greek word, *hagios*, means *set apart for God* — consecrated, belonging wholly to Him. It does not mean flawless and without sin. The saints we honor were not men and women without sin; they were sinners set apart and made holy by the grace of God. And that is the calling of every one of us who comes to this cup.

<OrnamentDivider variant="section" />

<SectionTitle numeral="XIV">Purgatory, War, and the Way of the Martyr</SectionTitle>

Since purgatory is named among these, let us say what it is. The word means the place where one's sins are *purged* after death, once the soul has departed from the body. And how did such a teaching arise? To put it plainly: **purgatory was invented to get crusaders out of hell.** It supplied a middle state, so that men who set out to kill in the name of Jesus, if they fell in battle, would not be damned outright but might somewhere atone for what they had done.

Afterward a whole superstructure of biblical proof-texts was built up to show that purgatory is in the Scriptures — chiefly **1 Corinthians 3**, where St. Paul says he laid a foundation and others build upon it:

<Scripture cite="1 Corinthians 3:13–15">
Each one's work will become clear; for the Day will declare it, because it will be revealed by fire; and the fire will test each one's work, of what sort it is… If anyone's work is burned, he will suffer loss; but he himself will be saved, yet so as through fire.
</Scripture>

They read this as sins being burned away in purgatory after death. But that is not what the Apostle means. To be "saved, yet so as through fire" is to be *preserved* in the fire — to burn and not be consumed — which speaks of hell, not of some purging halfway-house. (And the later notion of **toll houses** had no place in the Church of that age either.)

Why did the East never travel this road? Because the Orthodox had their bishops and their faithful in the very cities where the Muslims were; they lived side by side with them, suffered and were persecuted under them — and yet there was never any call to go and kill them all. For in Orthodoxy there is **no concept of a just war.** War is the lesser of two evils, the greatest evil on earth; you cannot justify the killing of any man. This is altogether unlike the West, where you read in Augustine's *City of God* the framework of the just war.

<Canon source="St. Basil the Great, Canon 13">
A soldier who has killed another man in battle is suspended from Holy Communion for three years. The taking of a human life is never regarded as a good thing — only, at most, as a necessary evil to defend the innocent from violence.
</Canon>

The Crusades, by contrast, were a summons to reclaim the holy places *at any cost*. The crusaders came down through Constantinople — so raucous a host that the emperor longed only to be rid of them — and at last, in the **Fourth Crusade**, when the Venetians went unpaid, they turned and sacked Constantinople itself. That was the straw that broke the back of the relations between the Latin West and the Orthodox East.

The way of Orthodoxy is rather the way of the **martyr**. St. Cyprian of Carthage, who lived through the Decian persecution and through the plague, when many were lapsing from the faith, counseled that the lapsed be given a chance to repent and return — and to those who clung to their lives he said, in effect: *You are afraid of dying? Where, then, is your faith? If we die, we go to a far better place. So what are we fighting for — a little patch of turf to hold a few more years? No: we are fighting for the Kingdom of God.* The only violence to which the Christian is called is the violence done against the devil, and against the sinful desires of his own heart.

Even our Lord, who overturned the tables in the temple, made a whip of cords to drive out the **animals** — the sheep and the oxen — as a shepherd drives his flock; it was never a whip laid upon men. There is no taking up of arms in Christ or in His apostles. The Christian conquers as the martyr conquers.
